Module 18, Q1: Opening Story — What happened after the New England farmland was abandoned, and what does this demonstrate?
After farmers abandoned cleared New England fields in the 1800s, the bare fields first filled with fast-growing weeds and goldenrod (r-selected), then were shaded out by eastern white pines, and eventually became mature broadleaf forests (oaks and maples). This demonstrates that populations and communities are dynamic—species constantly interact and alter their environment over time (ecological succession).
Module 18, Q2: What defines "a population", and how is it different from a community?
A population is composed of all individuals that belong to the same species and live in a given area at a particular time. A community incorporates all of the interacting populations of different species (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria) within that given area.
Module 18, Q3: How does the opening story demonstrate the importance of community-level analysis of ecology?
If scientists only looked at the goldenrod population in isolation, they couldn't explain why goldenrod declined. Only by studying the whole community—how tall pines blocked sunlight and how herbivorous insects and competitors interacted with goldenrod—can we explain why populations rise and fall.
Module 18, Q4: When considering a population as a system, what 2 processes are inputs (+) and what 2 processes are outputs (-)?
• Input 1 (+): Births (Natality)
• Input 2 (+): Immigration (individuals moving into the population)
• Output 1 (-): Deaths (Mortality)
• Output 2 (-): Emigration (individuals moving out of the population)
Module 18, Q5: Why is it important to know the population sex ratio and age structure?
Both help ecologists predict how fast a population will grow in the future. The sex ratio (especially the number of females) determines how many offspring can be produced in the next generation. The age structure shows what fraction of the population is pre-reproductive, reproductive, or post-reproductive—a population with many young individuals will grow rapidly.

Module 18, Q6: How can increasing population density act to limit that population's growth?
As population density increases (more individuals packed into the same area), competition for limiting resources (food, water, light, nest sites) intensifies, waste builds up, predators are attracted to the abundant prey, and infectious diseases/parasites spread much faster—raising death rates and lowering birth rates.
Module 18, Q7: How do density-dependent and density-independent factors affect a population's size? Provide examples of each.
• Density-Dependent Factors: Influence an individual's probability of survival and reproduction in a manner that depends on the size/density of the population (worse when crowded). Examples: Limited food supply, infectious disease, parasites, predation, territorial nesting space.
• Density-Independent Factors: Have the same effect on an individual's probability of survival at any population size (does not care how crowded it is). Examples: Tornadoes, hurricanes, floods, wildfires, volcanic eruptions, extreme freezes.

Module 18, Q8: What are typical limiting resources for plants? For animals?
• For Terrestrial Plants: Water, sunlight, space, and soil nutrients (especially nitrogen and phosphorus).
• For Animals: Food, water, shelter, and nesting/breeding sites.

Module 18, Q9: What is the relationship between limiting factors for a population and its environment's carrying capacity?
Limiting resources directly determine the carrying capacity (K)! The scarcest limiting resource sets the maximum ceiling (K) on how many individuals that environment can sustainably support. If a limiting resource increases (e.g., more food or rainfall), carrying capacity (K) increases; if it decreases, K drops.
Module 19, Q1: What does the intrinsic growth rate (r) of a species measure? How does it vary among species (r vs. K selection)?
The intrinsic growth rate (r) is the maximum potential rate for growth of a population under ideal conditions with unlimited resources. It varies dramatically by life-history strategy: small, short-lived r-selected species (like mice, insects, dandelions) have a very high r, while large, long-lived K-selected species (like elephants, whales, humans) have a low r.
Module 19, Q2: What occurs in logistic growth when a growing population approaches the carrying capacity (K)?
As the population approaches carrying capacity (K), limiting resources become scarce, so the growth rate slows down (decelerates) until births equal deaths and population growth levels off near zero (forming the flat top of the S-shaped curve).
Module 19, Q3: Can populations exceed carrying capacity in the short term? In the long term? What happens if they do?
• Short Term: YES (called an Overshoot), because of reproductive time lag (offspring conceived when food was plentiful are born after resources run low).
• Long Term: NO, because the environment lacks enough food/resources to sustain the excess population, triggering a rapid population crash called a Die-off (starvation, disease, emigration).
Module 19, Q4: In Figure 19.4, why doesn't the population stay steady at the level of the carrying capacity?
Because of time delays in how populations respond to resource changes and environmental fluctuations. The population overshoots K, overgrazes/depletes its food, crashes below K (die-off), allows vegetation to recover, and then rebounds—creating oscillations around carrying capacity rather than a flat line.
Module 19, Q5: How do predator-prey dynamics influence the populations of BOTH predators and prey? (Figure 19.5)
They lock both species into coupled oscillations: when prey numbers rise, predators have abundant food and their population grows after a brief time lag. Increased predation then drives prey numbers down, causing predators to starve and decline, which in turn allows the prey population to recover and repeat the cycle.
Module 19, Q6: Which types of species are better able to survive environmental change — r- or K-selected? Explain.
r-selected species are much better able to survive rapid environmental change! Because they reproduce quickly, have short generation times, produce huge numbers of offspring, and disperse widely, they can rapidly colonize disturbed habitats and evolve/adapt much faster than slow-breeding K-selected species.
Module 19, Q7: How do r- and K-selection influence survivorship curves?
• K-selected species invest heavily in parental care and protection of a few offspring, producing a Type I survivorship curve (high survival throughout youth/middle age; mortality concentrated in old age).
• r-selected species produce thousands of tiny offspring with little/no parental care, producing a Type III survivorship curve (massive early mortality; very few survive to adulthood).
Module 19, Q8: What is a metapopulation, and why is it important to study them?
A metapopulation is a group of spatially distinct populations that are connected by occasional movements of individuals between them (often via habitat corridors). Studying them is vital because human habitat fragmentation chops ecosystems into isolated patches; connected metapopulations provide genetic diversity (preventing inbreeding depression) and allow individuals from healthy patches to recolonize patches where a local population died out.
Module 19, Q9 & Q10 (Science Applied): How can human action cause some species to become overabundant, why is it harmful, and how do control options differ?
• Q9 (Cause & Harm): Humans eliminate top predators (wolves, cougars), fragment forests into edge habitats, and provide abundant food/trash/crops—causing species like white-tailed deer, suburban geese, or rats to explode. This harms ecosystems by overgrazing forest understories, spreading Lyme disease, and causing car collisions.
• Q10 (Control Options): In rural areas, regulated hunting or predator reintroduction works; in urban/suburban areas, hunting with firearms is unsafe, so cities use contraception/sterilization, trap-and-relocate, or sharp-shooters. Wealthy countries can afford costly wildlife fertility control and corridor engineering, whereas poorer regions rely more on physical barriers, community hunting, or low-cost habitat management.
